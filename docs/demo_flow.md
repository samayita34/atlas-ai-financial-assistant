Atlas AI Financial Assistant — Complete Demo Flow
1. Opening Hook (first 10-15 seconds)

Don't say "Hi, this is my project." Start mid-action — show the assistant already doing something, unprompted.

Screen recording opens on Telegram, already showing a message that just arrived from Atlas — not typed by you.

Atlas (unprompted, timestamped that morning): "Morning. Nvidia beat earnings expectations but guidance came in soft — that's why the stock is down 4% pre-market despite the beat. Since you're tracking semis, this matters more than the headline earnings number. Nothing else on your watchlist needs your attention today."

Voiceover line:

"This is Atlas. Nobody asked it anything. It just knows what matters to me, explains why, and — just as important — it didn't send five other things that don't matter. That's the whole philosophy of this product."

This single moment does more work than any slide: it shows proactivity, personalization, reasoning ("why"), and restraint (silence as a feature) — four of the five judging criteria — before you've said a word.

2. Demo Script (narration + beats, full walkthrough)

Use this as your spoken track under the screen recording. Each beat maps to a judging criterion so nothing is wasted motion.

Hook — proactive brief, unprompted (proves proactivity + personalization)
Onboarding glimpse — 15 seconds showing how naturally it captured preferences (proves product thinking)
Natural conversation + memory — ask something that only makes sense if it remembers you (proves AI experience)
Ambiguity handling — deliberately vague question, watch it ask back instead of guessing (proves judgment, not just chat)
Live financial research — real company question, grounded answer with sources, no hallucination (proves finance depth)
Document intelligence — upload a real report, ask a sharp question (proves finance depth + engineering)
Voice input — send a voice note, get a natural reply (proves multimodal, feels "alive")
Closing pitch — tie back to the brief's own language ("feels alive, watches, learns, acts")

Keep every exchange on-screen for 4-6 seconds max before cutting — judges lose interest in scrollback, not in pace.

4. Voice Input Example

Setup: Show yourself sending a voice message in Telegram (5-8 seconds, casual, not scripted-sounding).

[Voice note, transcribed on screen as it plays]: "Hey, can you check if there's any big news on Apple today, I've got a call with someone about it in like twenty minutes"

Atlas (text reply, fast): "Nothing major broke on Apple today — no filings, no earnings, no analyst downgrades. Only notable thing: a minor supplier report out of Asia that could affect iPhone production timing, but it's speculative. Should be a quiet call. Want a 3-line summary you can glance at right before you go in?"

Why this matters for the demo: it shows the assistant picked up urgency ("call in twenty minutes") from natural speech and adjusted its response to be fast and action-shaped, not because you told it to — it inferred that from context. That's a strong "feels intelligent" moment.

5. Financial Research Example

Setup: A pointed, real question — not a generic "tell me about Apple."

[You]: why did nvidia drop today even though earnings beat?

Atlas: "Earnings beat on revenue and EPS, but next-quarter guidance came in below what the Street was pricing in — that's almost always the bigger driver for a stock like Nvidia, where expectations were already sky-high. A few analysts also flagged margin pressure from ramping new chip production. Source: earnings release + same-day analyst notes. Want me to pull how the stock reacted after the last two guidance misses for comparison?"

Why this works: explains why, not just what — cites source type, offers a next step, and stays under 60 words. This single answer demonstrates finance depth (15%) and AI experience (20%) simultaneously.

6. Document Upload Example

Setup: Upload a real 10-Q or earnings deck PDF live, on screen.

[You, after uploading]: what's the biggest risk in here

Atlas: "The clearest flag is a jump in inventory relative to revenue growth — inventory is up 22% while revenue only grew 9%. That usually means either demand is softening or they overproduced. There's also a new line about supply chain concentration risk in Southeast Asia that wasn't in last quarter's filing. Want me to compare this to last quarter's report to see if this is a new or growing issue?"

Follow up live:

[You]: yes compare it

Atlas: "Compared to last quarter: inventory grew from 14% to 22% of revenue — that's a meaningful jump, not noise. The supply chain risk language is new this quarter, not boilerplate carried over. Together these suggest management is flagging something real. Worth asking about on the next earnings call."

Why this works: no scrolling through the PDF, no "here's a summary" — it goes straight to what a real analyst would flag, and proactively offers the comparison that makes the answer useful instead of just informative.

7. Memory Demonstration

Setup: This should be a callback to something said 10+ minutes earlier in the same demo, or ideally something from onboarding, days before — ideally recorded as a separate earlier session so the timestamp gap is visible on screen.

[Earlier, during onboarding — timestamp visible]: "Atlas: What sectors or companies are you following most closely?" "You: mostly semis, and I'm long on Nvidia"

[Later, different day — timestamp visible]: [You]: any big tech news today?

Atlas: "Nothing sector-wide worth flagging, but since you're long on Nvidia specifically, there's a new export policy proposal out of Washington that could affect chip sales to China — worth watching even though it hasn't moved the stock yet."

Why this works: it never asked "which stock do you mean" — it already knew, from a conversation days earlier, and surfaced something relevant because of a preference it stored, unprompted. This is the single hardest thing for judges to see in other teams' bots — most will reset context every session.

8. Personalized Daily Briefing Demonstration

Setup: Show two different simulated user profiles receiving two different briefings at the same time, to prove personalization isn't a template with find/replace.

User A (semis-focused analyst), 8:00 AM: "Morning. Nvidia guidance came in soft despite an earnings beat — that's the story today. Two smaller semis on your watchlist were flat, nothing else worth flagging."

User B (macro/founder persona), 8:00 AM: "Morning. Fed commentary yesterday hinted at a slower pace of cuts — that's likely to matter more for your runway/fundraising conversations than any single stock move today. Nothing else stands out."

Why this works: same feature, two structurally different outputs based on stored role and interests — this single side-by-side is the fastest way to prove "personalization" isn't cosmetic. Also demonstrate the silence principle explicitly: show a day where the brief is just — "Nothing significant to report today." — and narrate that this restraint is deliberate, not a missing feature.

9. Closing Pitch (30-45 seconds, spoken)

"Every team in this hackathon can wrap an LLM in a Telegram bot. That's not what Atlas is. Atlas remembers you, tells you why something matters instead of just what happened, knows when to stay quiet, and asks before it assumes. It doesn't feel like a chatbot you interrogate — it feels like an analyst who already did the reading and is waiting to tell you the one thing you actually need to know. That's the assistant the brief asked for: something that watches, learns, and acts — and we built exactly that, not a longer feature list."

Close on the hook message replaying on screen one more time — bookend the demo the way it opened.

12. Judge Q&A Preparation

Prepare tight, confident answers — 2-3 sentences each, no hedging.

"How do you prevent hallucinated financial data?" → "Every research and live-data answer is grounded in a live API call, not model memory — if the assistant can't confirm something from a source, it says so explicitly rather than guessing. That was a hard product rule from day one, not a fallback."

"How does memory actually work — is it just chat history in the prompt?" → "No — preferences, watchlists, and key facts are extracted and stored structurally in the database, not just replayed as raw chat history. That's what lets it recall something from onboarding days later without re-reading the whole conversation."

"Why did you skip Gmail/Calendar/Sheets integrations?" → "The brief was explicit that judgment matters more than integration count. We chose to make one financial data pipeline and the document/memory experience excellent rather than spread thin across four shallow integrations that wouldn't hold up under real use."

"How do you decide when to stay silent on the daily brief?" → "We set a relevance bar — if nothing clears it, nothing gets sent. That was a deliberate product decision straight from the brief's own principle that quality beats frequency, and it's something most naive implementations get wrong by defaulting to 'always send something.'"

"What happens with a request you genuinely can't handle?" → "It says so plainly and offers the closest useful alternative, instead of faking confidence — that's consistent with how it handles uncertain financial data too."

"Is this production-ready?" → "No, and the brief was explicit that it doesn't need to be — this is a focused demonstration of the product experience and reasoning quality, not a hardened system. The architecture is modular enough to extend, but polish went into the experience, not scale."

"What would you build next with more time?" → "Deeper watchlist-triggered alerts, one productivity integration done properly — likely Calendar for meeting prep — and expanding the personalization loop so it updates preferences from behavior, not just explicit statements."

13. Key Differentiators Over a Normal Chatbot

Use this as a mental checklist while demoing — every one of these should be visible, not claimed:

Speaks before being spoken to. Proactive, unprompted intelligence — not just a request/response loop.
Knows when to say nothing. Silence when nothing qualifies, instead of noise for its own sake.
Explains "why," not just "what." Every research answer carries reasoning and relevance, not a headline dump.
Asks before assuming. Clarifying questions on ambiguous requests instead of guessing and being wrong.
Remembers across time, not just within a session. Callbacks to onboarding or past conversations days later.
Different users get different outputs from the same feature. Personalization changes the actual content, not just a name token.
Admits uncertainty instead of fabricating. Explicit "I can't confirm this" moments, especially with financial data.
No menus, no commands, no buttons. Every interaction reads like a message to a colleague, never a UI to operate.
Multimodal without friction. Voice and documents are first-class inputs, not bolted-on demo tricks.
Concise by design. Every answer is a few sentences with a clear point, never a wall of text requiring scrolling.
